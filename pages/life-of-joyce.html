I · The Preface

James Joyce & his Dubliners

The man, the city and the book

Chapter IThe Author

Born on February 2nd 1882, James Joyce is considered one of Ireland’s most celebrated writers for his books, Dubliners and Ulysses. The eldest of 10 children, Joyce lived in Brighton Square, Rathgar with his parents, John Stanislaus Joyce and Mary Jane “May” (née Murray). Joyce attended Clongowes Wood College, Belvedere College and University College, formally located on St. Stephen’s Green, before moving to Paris to study medicine. Joyce returned to Ireland in April 1903 to attend to his family after the death of his mother and stayed until 1904. During this time, he met his future wife Nora Barnacle, from county Galway.

During this time Joyce also wrote his first short story for the magazine Irish Homestead. In October 1904, Joyce and Nora left Ireland to live in Pola, now known as Pula Croatia, where Joyce became an english teacher. After 1904, Joyce only visited Ireland three other times with his last visit being in 1912.

Life is too short to read a bad book.
James Joyce

Chapter IIAll About Dubliners

Published in 1914, Dubliners is one of James Joyce’s early works depicting a vignette of middle-class Irish life in Dublin. The book holds a collection of 15 stories that mimic the stages of life from childhood to death.

“to write a chapter of the moral history of my country”

Joyce’s intention behind the book

The stages of life

  1. I

    Childhood

    Stories that focus on themes of innocence, disillusionment and unsettling encounters.

    The Sisters · An Encounter · Araby

  2. II

    Adolescence

    The complexities of young adulthood, such as missed opportunities and one’s struggle for independence.

    Eveline · After the Race · Two Gallants · The Boarding House

  3. III

    Maturity

    The frustrations and mundanity of adulthood, characterised by unfulfilled ambitions and the characters’ personal failings.

    A Little Cloud · Counterparts · Clay · A Painful Case

  4. IV

    Public life

    A critical lens on Dublin’s social and political landscape, revealing its hypocrisy, stagnation and the vice-like grip Dublin’s past and Irish history have on the city and its people.

    Ivy Day in the Committee Room · A Mother · Grace

Coda

The final story in Dubliners, “The Dead”, is noted as the masterpiece of this collection and has been hailed by prominent literary figures, like T. S. Eliot, James Baldwin, and more.
